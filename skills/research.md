
# Research Skill

## Purpose

Conduct structured, evidence-based research to support strategic decisions.

The goal is not to collect as much information as possible. The goal is to obtain sufficient reliable evidence to understand the problem, identify relevant market conditions, and support subsequent strategic analysis.

## Principles

1. Evidence is more important than the number of sources.
2. Prefer primary sources over secondary sources.
3. Never invent facts, statistics, sources, URLs, quotations, or research findings.
4. Clearly distinguish verified facts, interpretations, assumptions, and hypotheses.
5. Search for contradictory evidence, not only evidence supporting the initial hypothesis.
6. Record the publication date and source URL whenever available.
7. Do not treat search snippets as sufficient evidence when the underlying source can be accessed.
8. Do not claim that a source was read if it was not actually accessed.
9. Avoid repeating the same information from multiple sources that depend on one original publication.
10. Stop researching when the available evidence is sufficient for the decision, not when an arbitrary number of sources has been reached.

## Phase 1. Define the Research Question

Before searching, identify:

- The strategic problem.
- The decision that research should support.
- The company, product, industry, or market being investigated.
- The relevant geography.
- The target audience or customer segment.
- The time period.
- Known constraints.
- Questions that must be answered.

If essential information is missing, state assumptions explicitly.

Do not begin broad research without defining its purpose.

## Phase 2. Build a Research Plan

Divide the research into relevant areas.

Possible areas include:

- Market size and dynamics.
- Customer needs and behavior.
- Customer pain points.
- Competitors.
- Alternative solutions.
- Industry trends.
- Technology.
- Regulation.
- Business models.
- Pricing.
- Distribution.
- Barriers to entry.
- Risks.
- Opportunities.

Not every project requires every research area.

Prioritize questions based on their expected impact on the strategic decision.

For each research question, define:

- Question.
- Why it matters.
- Potential sources.
- What evidence would answer it.
- What evidence would contradict the current hypothesis.

The research plan must be created before substantial information collection.

## Phase 3. Search and Collect Sources

Use multiple search queries with different formulations.

Search for:

- Official company and industry information.
- Government statistics.
- Industry reports.
- Academic publications.
- Competitor websites.
- Product documentation.
- Customer reviews.
- Interviews.
- News publications.
- Public datasets.

Prefer sources in the following order when applicable:

1. Primary data and original research.
2. Official statistics and regulatory documents.
3. Original company disclosures.
4. Peer-reviewed research.
5. Reputable industry reports.
6. Reliable journalism.
7. Expert commentary.
8. Community discussions and reviews.

This hierarchy is contextual. A customer review may be more useful for understanding a customer pain point than an official company report.

## Phase 4. Evaluate Source Quality

Evaluate each source using the following criteria:

- Authority: Who published the information?
- Proximity: Is the source directly responsible for the information?
- Recency: Is the information current enough?
- Methodology: Is the method of obtaining the information transparent?
- Relevance: Does it answer the research question?
- Independence: Is it independent from other cited sources?
- Limitations: What biases or limitations exist?

Assign a qualitative source rating:

- High: authoritative, directly relevant, transparent, and independently verifiable.
- Medium: credible but with limitations in methodology, relevance, or independence.
- Low: weakly supported, unclear provenance, potentially biased, or insufficiently verifiable.

A low-quality source may still provide a useful lead, but it must not be treated as strong evidence.

## Phase 5. Extract Evidence

For every meaningful finding, record:

- Claim ID.
- Claim.
- Evidence.
- Source title.
- Source URL.
- Publication date.
- Access date, if available.
- Source type.
- Source quality.
- Confidence.
- Limitations.
- Relevance to the strategic decision.

Separate:

### Fact

Directly supported by a source.

### Interpretation

A conclusion derived from one or more facts.

### Hypothesis

A proposition that has not yet been sufficiently verified.

### Unknown

An important question for which evidence has not been obtained.

Never silently convert an interpretation or hypothesis into a fact.

## Phase 6. Verify Important Claims

For claims that materially affect strategic decisions:

- Seek independent corroboration.
- Check the original source.
- Verify numbers, dates, geography, and definitions.
- Check whether the evidence is outdated.
- Look for conflicting findings.
- Identify whether the source supports the claim exactly as written.

If a claim cannot be verified, lower its confidence or mark it as unverified.

If sources contradict each other:

1. Record both findings.
2. Compare methodologies and definitions.
3. Compare publication dates.
4. Identify possible reasons for the discrepancy.
5. Do not force an artificial consensus.

## Phase 7. Identify Research Gaps

After collecting evidence, identify:

- Questions that remain unanswered.
- Missing customer or market data.
- Claims supported by only one weak source.
- Conflicting evidence.
- Potentially outdated information.
- Assumptions requiring validation.
- Research that would materially change the strategic decision.

Prioritize gaps by:

- Strategic importance.
- Uncertainty.
- Cost of being wrong.
- Feasibility of obtaining additional evidence.

## Phase 8. Research Completion Criteria

Research is complete when:

- The primary research questions have been addressed.
- Important strategic claims have sufficient evidence.
- Significant contradictory evidence has been considered.
- Critical uncertainties are documented.
- Further research is unlikely to materially change the next strategic decision.

Do not declare research complete solely because a predefined number of sources or claims has been collected.

## Output

Produce a structured research report containing:

1. Research objective.
2. Research questions.
3. Research methodology.
4. Key findings.
5. Market and customer evidence.
6. Competitive evidence.
7. Relevant trends.
8. Contradictions.
9. Research gaps.
10. Strategic implications.
11. Sources.

Maintain a separate evidence ledger when required by the project workflow.

## Handoff

Provide the analysis stage with:

- Verified facts.
- Evidence-backed claims.
- Source references.
- Confidence levels.
- Contradictions.
- Research gaps.
- Hypotheses.
- Potential strategic implications.

Research must not make the final strategic decision.

Its responsibility is to establish the evidence base for subsequent stages.
