
# Options Skill

## Purpose

Generate, develop, and compare distinct strategic alternatives based on research and analysis.

The goal is to identify credible alternative paths for achieving the strategic objective, evaluate their trade-offs, and provide a structured basis for strategic selection.

The options stage must not simply produce several variations of the same strategy.

## Core Principles

1. Every option must address the same strategic objective.
2. Options must differ in their underlying strategic logic.
3. Every option must be grounded in research and analysis.
4. Do not invent market facts, financial estimates, or capabilities.
5. Clearly distinguish evidence, assumptions, and projections.
6. Every option must be realistically implementable or explicitly identified as exploratory.
7. Evaluate all options using consistent criteria.
8. Include risks, disadvantages, and opportunity costs.
9. Do not automatically prefer the most ambitious, innovative, or highest-growth option.
10. Do not select the final strategy at this stage.

## Mandatory baseline alternatives and claim vocabulary

Every options report must include these alternatives explicitly, not merely mention them in a note:

### Status quo — maintain the current state
Describe what happens if the organization does not make the proposed strategic change. Include the likely consequences of inaction, ongoing costs, and opportunity costs. Do not portray it as automatically safe or neutral.

### Kill — stop or close the direction
Describe the deliberate decision to stop the initiative, product direction, or proposed investment. Specify what is stopped, obligations that remain, exit/transition costs, customer and operational consequences, and resources that may be released. If a full shutdown is not applicable, explain the narrowest meaningful scope that can be stopped. Do not invent savings or liabilities.

Status quo and Kill are separate alternatives. They do not count as the active strategic option. Unless the brief explicitly changes scope and the human approves that change, provide at least one active alternative as well (minimum three options total).

### Canonical claims statuses

Use only the following machine-readable values in state/claims.jsonl, matching workflow.md:

- fact — directly supported by an inspectable source or reproducible calculation.
- hypothesis — unverified proposition or interpretation requiring validation.
- forecast — forward-looking estimate or projection with assumptions.
- outdated — previously recorded claim no longer current; preserve the record.
- retracted — withdrawn because it was erroneous or no longer defensible; preserve the record and reason.

Do not substitute labels such as verified, assumption, прогноз, or confirmed in the claims status field. Put confidence in the separate confidence field and explain assumptions/limitations in notes. A source-quality rating is not a claim status.

## Input

The options stage receives:

- Project brief.
- Strategic objective.
- Research report.
- Evidence ledger.
- Analytical report.
- Prioritized insights.
- Customer segments.
- Opportunities.
- Competitive gaps.
- Company capabilities.
- Constraints.
- Risks.
- Hypotheses.
- Unresolved questions.

If critical information is missing, document the assumptions required to generate alternatives.

## Phase 1. Define the Strategic Decision

Clearly specify:

- What decision must be made?
- What objective must be achieved?
- What constraints must be respected?
- What time horizon is relevant?
- What resources are available?
- What outcomes would constitute success?

All strategic alternatives must address the same decision and objective.

## Phase 2. Establish Strategic Directions

Identify fundamentally different ways to achieve the objective.

Depending on the project, directions may include:

### Market Strategy

- Enter a new market.
- Expand within the existing market.
- Focus on a niche.
- Target a new customer segment.
- Expand geographically.

### Product Strategy

- Improve the existing product.
- Develop new products.
- Create complementary products.
- Simplify the product portfolio.
- Develop a premium or specialized offering.

### Growth Strategy

- Organic growth.
- Partnerships.
- Distribution expansion.
- Acquisition.
- Platform development.
- Ecosystem development.

### Business Model Strategy

- Change pricing.
- Introduce subscriptions.
- Monetize new services.
- Change distribution economics.
- Shift from product to service.
- Introduce a different revenue model.

### Positioning Strategy

- Cost leadership.
- Differentiation.
- Specialization.
- Premium positioning.
- Convenience.
- Reliability.
- Customer experience.

These directions are examples, not mandatory categories.

Choose strategic directions relevant to the specific project.

## Phase 3. Generate Strategic Alternatives

Generate multiple possible directions before selecting the strongest alternatives for detailed development.

Avoid prematurely converging on the first attractive idea.

Each alternative must have a distinct strategic logic.

A useful alternative should differ from other alternatives in at least one fundamental dimension:

- Target customer.
- Value proposition.
- Competitive positioning.
- Product or service.
- Revenue model.
- Distribution.
- Required capabilities.
- Resource allocation.
- Growth mechanism.
- Risk profile.

If two alternatives differ only in implementation details, combine them or explain why the difference is strategically meaningful.

## Phase 4. Develop Three Core Options

Unless the project requires a different number, develop three strategic alternatives.

The alternatives should represent meaningfully different approaches.

For example:

### Option A — Focused Strategy

Concentrate resources on a narrow, clearly defined segment or opportunity.

Potential characteristics:

- Narrow target audience.
- Focused value proposition.
- Limited initial scope.
- Lower complexity.
- Potentially easier validation.

### Option B — Expansion Strategy

Pursue broader growth through additional segments, markets, channels, or offerings.

Potential characteristics:

- Larger potential market.
- More growth opportunities.
- Greater operational complexity.
- More resource requirements.
- Greater execution risk.

### Option C — Transformational Strategy

Change the underlying product, positioning, business model, or operating approach.

Potential characteristics:

- Potentially substantial differentiation.
- Higher uncertainty.
- Greater capability requirements.
- More significant organizational changes.
- Potentially greater long-term impact.

These archetypes must not be used mechanically.

Develop options that are relevant to the actual strategic context.

## Phase 5. Describe Each Option

For every alternative, provide:

### 1. Option Name

A concise, distinctive name.

### 2. Strategic Thesis

Explain the core logic of the option in one or two sentences.

### 3. Objective

Specify what the option is intended to achieve.

### 4. Target Audience

Identify the primary customer or market segment.

### 5. Value Proposition

Explain what value is offered and why the target audience would choose it.

### 6. Strategic Mechanism

Explain how the option is expected to achieve the objective.

### 7. Key Initiatives

Identify the major actions required.

### 8. Required Capabilities

Specify the resources, expertise, technology, partnerships, and operational capabilities required.

Distinguish capabilities already available from capabilities that must be developed or acquired.

### 9. Business Model

Describe how the option creates, delivers, and captures value.

Where relevant, specify:

- Revenue streams.
- Pricing logic.
- Cost structure.
- Distribution.
- Monetization.

### 10. Expected Outcomes

Describe the expected outcomes.

Separate:

- Evidence-supported expectations.
- Qualitative projections.
- Quantitative estimates.
- Speculative outcomes.

### 11. Assumptions

List the critical assumptions underlying the option.

### 12. Risks

Identify strategic, operational, financial, technological, competitive, regulatory, and adoption risks where relevant.

### 13. Limitations and Trade-offs

Explain what the organization sacrifices by choosing this option.

### 14. Implementation Complexity

Assess the complexity of execution and organizational change.

### 15. Time Horizon

Describe the expected implementation horizon.

Do not invent precise timelines when the necessary information is unavailable.

## Phase 6. Evaluate Options Consistently

Evaluate all alternatives using the same criteria.

Default evaluation criteria:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Strategic fit | How well does the option address the objective? |
| Market attractiveness | Is the relevant opportunity attractive? |
| Customer value | Does the option address important customer needs? |
| Differentiation | Can the organization establish a meaningful advantage? |
| Feasibility | Can the organization realistically execute it? |
| Economics | Is the expected economic outcome attractive? |
| Resource requirements | How much investment and effort are required? |
| Time to impact | How quickly could meaningful results emerge? |
| Risk | What is the likelihood and potential impact of failure? |
| Scalability | Can the approach grow sustainably? |
| Reversibility | Can the decision be adjusted if assumptions prove wrong? |

Not every criterion must have equal importance.

If weights are used:

- Explain why the weights were selected.
- Ensure the weights sum to 100%.
- Do not use false precision.
- Identify the source of the scoring assumptions.
- Perform sensitivity analysis when the ranking depends heavily on weights.

## Phase 7. Score Options

Use a consistent qualitative scale:

- 1 — Very weak.
- 2 — Weak.
- 3 — Moderate.
- 4 — Strong.
- 5 — Very strong.

Use this scale only when the available evidence supports meaningful comparison.

For each score, provide a concise justification.

If evidence is insufficient, use:

- Unknown.
- Insufficient evidence.
- Requires validation.

Do not assign a numerical score simply to fill the table.

Avoid presenting qualitative scores as objective measurements.

## Phase 8. Compare Trade-offs

Identify the fundamental trade-offs between alternatives.

Examples:

- Growth versus profitability.
- Speed versus quality.
- Focus versus breadth.
- Low investment versus high potential.
- Short-term returns versus long-term positioning.
- Control versus partnership.
- Stability versus experimentation.
- Existing capabilities versus capability development.

Explain why no option is universally superior.

Identify the conditions under which each option would be preferable.

## Phase 9. Identify Hybrid Options

Consider whether a combination of alternatives could produce a better outcome.

A hybrid option must have a coherent strategic logic.

Do not combine initiatives merely because they are individually attractive.

Evaluate:

- Compatibility.
- Resource conflicts.
- Execution complexity.
- Sequencing.
- Dependencies.
- Potential dilution of focus.

If a hybrid is recommended for consideration, explain what it combines and why.

## Phase 10. Identify Validation Opportunities

For each option, identify the critical assumptions that should be tested before major investment.

Possible validation methods:

- Customer interviews.
- Surveys.
- Landing-page tests.
- Prototypes.
- Pilots.
- Pricing experiments.
- Partnerships.
- Small-scale launches.
- Operational tests.

Specify:

- Hypothesis.
- Experiment.
- Metric.
- Success threshold.
- Failure threshold.
- Duration, if reasonably estimable.
- Decision resulting from the test.

Do not invent thresholds without a rationale.

If thresholds are unknown, propose how they should be established.

## Phase 11. Identify Decision Conditions

For each option, identify:

- Conditions that make it attractive.
- Conditions that make it unattractive.
- Critical assumptions.
- Evidence that could change its ranking.
- Potential reasons to reject it.
- Potential reasons to reconsider it in the future.

The comparison should help the decision-maker understand when each option is appropriate.

## Output

Produce a structured strategic alternatives report containing:

1. Strategic objective.
2. Decision context.
3. Strategic directions considered.
4. Three distinct strategic alternatives.
5. Detailed description of each option.
6. Comparative evaluation.
7. Scoring rationale.
8. Trade-offs.
9. Risks.
10. Required capabilities.
11. Resource implications.
12. Expected outcomes.
13. Critical assumptions.
14. Validation experiments.
15. Potential hybrid alternatives.
16. Decision conditions.
17. Summary of the strategic choice.

## Handoff

Provide the strategy stage with:

- Strategic objective.
- Three developed alternatives.
- Strategic theses.
- Evidence supporting each option.
- Comparative evaluation.
- Risks.
- Trade-offs.
- Required resources.
- Critical assumptions.
- Validation opportunities.
- Decision conditions.
- Uncertainties.

The options stage must not make the final decision.

Its responsibility is to develop a credible set of alternatives and make their differences, implications, and trade-offs transparent.
