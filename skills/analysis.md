
# Analysis Skill

## Purpose

Transform research evidence into actionable strategic insights.

The analysis stage must explain what the collected evidence means, why it matters, what implications it creates, and which uncertainties remain.

Analysis must not simply summarize research findings or prematurely prescribe a strategy.

## Core Principles

1. Separate facts, interpretations, hypotheses, and recommendations.
2. Every significant conclusion must be traceable to evidence or explicitly identified assumptions.
3. Do not invent quantitative estimates, causal relationships, or market characteristics.
4. Correlation does not establish causation.
5. Analyze evidence that contradicts the prevailing hypothesis.
6. Use analytical frameworks only when they help answer a specific question.
7. Avoid generic conclusions that could apply to any company or market.
8. Focus on implications for the actual strategic problem.
9. Distinguish what is known from what is uncertain.
10. Prioritize insights according to their strategic importance.

## Input

The analysis stage receives:

- Research report.
- Evidence ledger.
- Research questions.
- Project brief.
- Business context.
- Constraints.
- Identified research gaps.
- Existing assumptions.

If information is missing, identify it explicitly.

Do not silently invent missing business context.

## Phase 1. Establish the Analytical Context

Define:

- Strategic problem.
- Desired outcome.
- Current position.
- Relevant market.
- Target customers.
- Available resources.
- Constraints.
- Decision that must be made.

Identify the difference between:

- The stated problem.
- The underlying problem.
- Symptoms.
- Potential root causes.

Do not assume that the original problem statement correctly identifies the underlying problem.

## Phase 2. Organize the Evidence

Group research findings into relevant analytical categories.

Possible categories:

- Market dynamics.
- Customer needs.
- Customer behavior.
- Competitive environment.
- Company capabilities.
- Business model.
- Economics.
- Distribution.
- Technology.
- Regulation.
- Risks.
- Opportunities.

Use categories relevant to the project rather than applying all categories mechanically.

Identify:

- Strongly supported findings.
- Weakly supported findings.
- Contradictory findings.
- Missing evidence.
- Assumptions.

## Phase 3. Identify Patterns

Search for meaningful patterns across the evidence.

Examples:

- Repeated customer pain points.
- Changes in customer behavior.
- Underserved segments.
- Competitor weaknesses.
- Emerging market trends.
- Structural barriers.
- Differences between customer segments.
- Mismatches between customer expectations and existing offerings.
- Changes in value-chain economics.
- Opportunities created by technological or regulatory changes.

A pattern must be supported by sufficient evidence.

Do not treat an isolated observation as a market-wide pattern without justification.

## Phase 4. Analyze Customers

Where relevant, examine:

- Customer segments.
- Jobs to be done.
- Needs.
- Pain points.
- Desired outcomes.
- Current alternatives.
- Switching costs.
- Buying behavior.
- Decision-makers.
- Barriers to adoption.
- Willingness to pay.
- Frequency and urgency of the problem.

Distinguish:

- What customers say.
- What customers do.
- What customers are willing to pay for.
- What is inferred from available evidence.

Do not assume that expressed interest equals purchase intent.

## Phase 5. Analyze Competitors and Alternatives

Identify the relevant competitive landscape.

Consider:

- Direct competitors.
- Indirect competitors.
- Substitute solutions.
- The option of doing nothing.
- Internal or manual alternatives.

Compare competitors using criteria relevant to the strategic decision.

Possible criteria:

- Target audience.
- Value proposition.
- Product capabilities.
- Pricing.
- Distribution.
- Positioning.
- Strengths.
- Weaknesses.
- Barriers.
- Differentiation.

Avoid superficial comparisons based only on feature lists.

Identify areas where competitors are strong, where customer needs remain unmet, and where differentiation may be possible.

Do not assume that a competitor weakness automatically creates an attractive opportunity.

## Phase 6. Analyze the Business and Its Capabilities

Evaluate the relationship between the opportunity and available capabilities.

Consider:

- Resources.
- Expertise.
- Technology.
- Brand.
- Distribution.
- Partnerships.
- Operational capacity.
- Financial constraints.
- Organizational readiness.
- Competitive advantages.

Identify capabilities that are:

- Already available.
- Developable.
- Obtainable through partnerships.
- Difficult or expensive to acquire.

Distinguish an attractive market opportunity from an opportunity the organization can realistically pursue.

## Phase 7. Apply Analytical Frameworks When Useful

Choose frameworks according to the problem.

### SWOT

Use SWOT to summarize:

- Strengths.
- Weaknesses.
- Opportunities.
- Threats.

Each item must be grounded in project-specific evidence.

Avoid generic SWOT statements.

### PESTEL

Use PESTEL when external macro-environmental factors materially influence the strategic decision.

Consider:

- Political.
- Economic.
- Social.
- Technological.
- Environmental.
- Legal.

Include only relevant factors.

### Porter's Five Forces

Use when competitive structure and industry economics are important.

Analyze:

- Rivalry.
- Threat of new entrants.
- Threat of substitutes.
- Buyer power.
- Supplier power.

Explain how each force affects profitability or strategic positioning.

### Jobs to Be Done

Use when understanding customer motivations and desired outcomes is central.

Identify:

- Situation.
- Trigger.
- Desired progress.
- Functional needs.
- Emotional needs.
- Social needs.
- Existing solutions.
- Barriers.

### Segmentation

Use when customers have meaningfully different needs, behaviors, economics, or acquisition channels.

Segments must be distinguishable and strategically relevant.

### Value Chain

Use when the sources of value creation, costs, dependencies, or differentiation need to be identified.

### Other Frameworks

Additional frameworks may be used when appropriate.

Explain why the framework was selected.

Do not use frameworks merely to make the report appear comprehensive.

## Phase 8. Analyze Economics

When sufficient data exists, evaluate:

- Revenue drivers.
- Cost drivers.
- Pricing.
- Margins.
- Customer acquisition costs.
- Customer lifetime value.
- Retention.
- Payback period.
- Unit economics.
- Scalability.
- Capital requirements.

Use transparent assumptions.

If numerical inputs are unavailable:

- Do not fabricate calculations.
- Identify the missing inputs.
- Provide formulas where useful.
- Use scenarios only when assumptions are explicitly stated.
- Label illustrative calculations as illustrative.

Do not present hypothetical calculations as actual business performance.

## Phase 9. Identify Strategic Insights

An insight must go beyond describing an observation.

For each insight, explain:

1. Evidence: What was observed?
2. Interpretation: What does it mean?
3. Implication: Why does it matter?
4. Strategic relevance: How might it influence a decision?
5. Confidence: How strongly is it supported?
6. Uncertainty: What could invalidate it?

A useful insight should help the organization make a decision, change a priority, or reconsider an assumption.

Avoid statements such as:

- "The market is competitive."
- "Customers want convenience."
- "Technology is developing rapidly."

Unless these observations are connected to a specific, evidence-backed strategic implication.

## Phase 10. Identify Strategic Problems and Opportunities

Identify:

- Core strategic challenges.
- Root causes.
- Structural constraints.
- Unmet needs.
- Attractive opportunities.
- Competitive vulnerabilities.
- Potential sources of differentiation.
- Important trade-offs.

Prioritize them using criteria such as:

- Strategic impact.
- Evidence strength.
- Urgency.
- Feasibility.
- Potential value.
- Risk.
- Reversibility.

Do not prioritize opportunities solely because they appear large.

## Phase 11. Develop Hypotheses

Translate important insights into testable hypotheses.

A hypothesis should specify:

- The proposition.
- The rationale.
- Supporting evidence.
- Assumptions.
- What would confirm it.
- What would disprove it.
- How it could be tested.
- The potential strategic consequence.

Example:

Hypothesis:
A narrowly focused product for a specific customer segment may outperform a broad product offering.

Rationale:
The research indicates that this segment has a distinct, recurring problem and limited suitable alternatives.

Assumptions:
The segment is sufficiently large, reachable, and willing to pay.

Validation:
Conduct customer interviews, test a landing page, or run a pilot.

Do not treat hypotheses as established facts.

## Phase 12. Prioritize Findings

Rank insights according to their relevance to the strategic decision.

Consider:

- Magnitude of potential impact.
- Confidence in evidence.
- Relevance to the objective.
- Time sensitivity.
- Feasibility.
- Cost of being wrong.

Clearly distinguish high-impact/high-confidence findings from speculative opportunities.

## Output

Produce an analytical report containing:

1. Executive analytical summary.
2. Problem definition.
3. Context and constraints.
4. Evidence synthesis.
5. Customer analysis.
6. Competitive analysis.
7. Business and capability analysis.
8. Relevant framework results.
9. Economic analysis, if possible.
10. Key insights.
11. Root causes.
12. Strategic challenges.
13. Opportunities.
14. Hypotheses.
15. Prioritized findings.
16. Uncertainties and research gaps.
17. Implications for strategy development.

## Handoff

Provide the options stage with:

- Strategic problem.
- Desired outcome.
- Prioritized opportunities.
- Key insights.
- Customer segments.
- Unmet needs.
- Competitive gaps.
- Available capabilities.
- Constraints.
- Hypotheses.
- Risks.
- Evidence references.
- Confidence levels.
- Unresolved questions.

The analysis stage must not select the final strategy.

Its responsibility is to establish what strategic choices are available and what evidence supports them.
