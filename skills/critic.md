
# Critic Skill

## Purpose

Independently evaluate a proposed strategy and identify weaknesses that could undermine its validity, feasibility, effectiveness, or implementation.

The critic acts as an adversarial reviewer.

Its objective is to improve strategic decision-making by challenging assumptions, identifying unsupported claims, testing strategic logic, examining risks, and searching for credible alternative explanations.

The critic must not rewrite the strategy or automatically approve it.

## Core Principles

1. Evaluate the strategy independently from its author.
2. Do not assume the strategy is correct.
3. Challenge the reasoning, not the writing style.
4. Prioritize material weaknesses over minor imperfections.
5. Do not invent problems, evidence, or risks.
6. Every criticism must contain a clear rationale.
7. Distinguish factual errors from analytical weaknesses and strategic disagreements.
8. Identify evidence supporting the strategy as well as evidence contradicting it.
9. Evaluate the strategy against its stated objective and constraints.
10. Do not use hindsight or information unavailable to the original decision-maker unless explicitly conducting an updated review.
11. Do not confuse uncertainty with evidence of failure.
12. Do not recommend changes without explaining why they improve the strategy.
13. Do not modify the original strategy.
14. Do not soften critical findings merely to make the assessment agreeable.
15. Do not exaggerate weaknesses to appear rigorous.

## Input

The critic receives:

- Project brief.
- Strategic objective.
- Research report.
- Evidence ledger.
- Analytical report.
- Strategic alternatives.
- Selected strategy.
- Decision rationale.
- Roadmap.
- KPIs.
- Resource estimates.
- Risks.
- Constraints.
- Assumptions.

If supporting evidence is unavailable, identify the limitation.

Do not assume access to sources or data that were not provided.

## Phase 1. Understand the Strategy

Before criticizing, reconstruct the strategy's logic.

Identify:

- Strategic objective.
- Target audience.
- Market.
- Value proposition.
- Strategic positioning.
- Chosen direction.
- Competitive advantage.
- Strategic pillars.
- Major initiatives.
- Required capabilities.
- Resource allocation.
- Expected outcomes.
- Critical assumptions.
- Risks.
- Implementation roadmap.

Summarize the strategy in a concise statement.

If the strategy cannot be summarized coherently, identify the underlying ambiguity.

## Phase 2. Evaluate Strategic Logic

Examine whether the strategy has a logical connection between:

1. Problem.
2. Evidence.
3. Insights.
4. Strategic choice.
5. Value proposition.
6. Initiatives.
7. Expected outcomes.

Identify broken links.

Ask:

- Does the selected strategy actually solve the stated problem?
- Are the proposed actions logically connected to the objective?
- Are the expected outcomes plausible given the actions?
- Are important causal relationships assumed rather than demonstrated?
- Does the strategy depend on contradictory assumptions?
- Are the strategic priorities mutually compatible?
- Does the strategy contain unnecessary initiatives?
- Is the strategy sufficiently focused?

Identify the weakest links in the strategic logic.

## Phase 3. Challenge the Evidence

Review the evidence supporting major strategic decisions.

Examine:

- Source reliability.
- Relevance.
- Recency.
- Independence.
- Methodology.
- Representativeness.
- Sample limitations.
- Definitions.
- Geographic relevance.
- Potential bias.

Identify:

- Unsupported claims.
- Overgeneralizations.
- Weak evidence.
- Outdated evidence.
- Conflicting findings.
- Missing evidence.
- Misinterpretation of sources.
- Conclusions stronger than the underlying evidence.

For every material evidence problem, identify the affected strategic decision.

Do not treat every missing citation as a critical strategic flaw.

Prioritize evidence gaps that could change the decision.

## Phase 4. Challenge Critical Assumptions

Identify the assumptions on which the strategy depends.

Examples:

- Customers will adopt the proposed solution.
- Customers are willing to pay.
- The market is sufficiently large.
- Competitors will not respond aggressively.
- The organization can acquire required capabilities.
- Costs will remain within acceptable limits.
- The proposed distribution channel will work.
- Regulatory conditions will remain favorable.
- The proposed timeline is realistic.

For each critical assumption, evaluate:

- Why it is necessary.
- Evidence supporting it.
- Evidence contradicting it.
- Confidence.
- Consequence if it is false.
- How it could be tested.

Distinguish critical assumptions from minor assumptions.

## Phase 5. Examine Customer and Market Risks

Challenge the understanding of customers and market demand.

Ask:

- Is the target segment clearly defined?
- Is the customer problem sufficiently important?
- Is the proposed solution meaningfully better than existing alternatives?
- Is the customer willing to pay?
- Is the target market accessible?
- Are switching costs understood?
- Is customer behavior supported by evidence?
- Is the market size based on realistic assumptions?
- Is the market growing, shrinking, or changing in ways that matter?
- Are important segments being overlooked?

Identify plausible reasons why customers may reject the offering.

## Phase 6. Examine Competitive Risks

Evaluate the strategy against:

- Direct competitors.
- Indirect competitors.
- Substitute products.
- Existing customer behavior.
- New entrants.
- Potential competitive responses.

Ask:

- Can the proposed advantage be copied?
- Is differentiation meaningful to customers?
- Does the strategy depend on competitors remaining passive?
- Could competitors respond through pricing, distribution, features, partnerships, or marketing?
- Are there overlooked substitutes?
- Is the proposed positioning credible?
- Is the competitive advantage sustainable?

Identify the strongest plausible competitive response.

## Phase 7. Examine Feasibility

Evaluate whether the organization can realistically execute the strategy.

Consider:

- Capabilities.
- Personnel.
- Expertise.
- Technology.
- Infrastructure.
- Partnerships.
- Operational capacity.
- Management capacity.
- Financial resources.
- Time.
- Dependencies.

Ask:

- Are required capabilities available?
- Are capability gaps underestimated?
- Are initiatives competing for the same resources?
- Is the implementation sequence realistic?
- Are dependencies properly identified?
- Does the roadmap assume too much can happen simultaneously?
- Are responsibilities clearly defined?
- Are operational constraints acknowledged?

Identify the most significant execution bottlenecks.

## Phase 8. Examine Economic Viability

Where sufficient data exists, challenge the economic assumptions.

Consider:

- Revenue assumptions.
- Pricing.
- Demand.
- Acquisition costs.
- Retention.
- Margins.
- Operating costs.
- Investment.
- Cash requirements.
- Payback.
- Scalability.

Ask:

- Are projections supported by evidence?
- Are costs underestimated?
- Are revenue assumptions optimistic?
- Are unit economics plausible?
- Does the strategy depend on unrealistic growth?
- Is the business model sustainable?
- Are downside scenarios considered?

Do not invent financial figures.

When data is insufficient, identify the missing information and the potential consequences.

## Phase 9. Examine Risks and Failure Modes

Identify credible failure scenarios.

Consider:

- Market failure.
- Customer adoption failure.
- Competitive response.
- Financial failure.
- Execution failure.
- Technology failure.
- Regulatory changes.
- Operational disruption.
- Partnership failure.
- Reputational damage.
- Strategic drift.

For each major failure scenario, describe:

1. Scenario.
2. Trigger.
3. Why it is plausible.
4. Potential impact.
5. Early warning signs.
6. Existing mitigation.
7. Remaining exposure.

Prioritize risks that could invalidate the strategy or materially reduce its expected value.

## Phase 10. Challenge KPIs and Measurement

Examine whether the measurement system supports the strategic objective.

Ask:

- Are KPIs correctly defined?
- Do they measure outcomes or merely activity?
- Are leading indicators included?
- Are baselines available?
- Are targets realistic?
- Are targets evidence-based?
- Can metrics be manipulated?
- Could optimizing one metric harm another?
- Are guardrail metrics included?
- Is measurement frequency appropriate?
- Are data sources reliable?

Identify misleading, incomplete, or missing metrics.

## Phase 11. Examine Alternatives and Opportunity Cost

Evaluate whether the selected strategy was chosen against credible alternatives.

Ask:

- Were materially different alternatives considered?
- Were alternatives evaluated consistently?
- Were rejected options dismissed for valid reasons?
- Is the chosen strategy genuinely superior under the stated constraints?
- What opportunity is sacrificed?
- Could a smaller, cheaper, or more reversible strategy achieve similar outcomes?
- Could a hybrid approach be more effective?
- Is the organization committing too early?

Identify the strongest alternative strategy.

Explain under which conditions it could outperform the selected strategy.

## Phase 12. Construct a Counterstrategy

Develop a credible alternative that challenges the current strategy.

The counterstrategy must:

- Address the same objective.
- Be grounded in the available evidence.
- Use a different strategic logic.
- Respect relevant constraints.
- Explain its advantages.
- Explain its disadvantages.
- Identify the conditions under which it would be preferable.

The purpose is not necessarily to recommend the counterstrategy.

It is to test whether the selected strategy is robust against alternative approaches.

## Phase 13. Assess Robustness

Evaluate how the strategy performs under different scenarios.

Where relevant, consider:

### Base Scenario

Conditions remain broadly consistent with current assumptions.

### Downside Scenario

Demand, resources, economics, competition, or implementation conditions are worse than expected.

### Upside Scenario

Conditions are more favorable than expected.

Consider:

- Does the strategy remain viable?
- Which assumptions become critical?
- Which initiatives should be delayed?
- Which investments should be reduced?
- Which opportunities should be accelerated?
- What decisions should change under each scenario?

Do not create unsupported numerical forecasts.

## Phase 14. Classify Findings

Classify every significant finding.

### Critical

A weakness that could invalidate the strategy, cause unacceptable losses, or make successful execution highly unlikely.

### Major

A weakness that materially reduces strategic effectiveness or creates significant implementation risk.

### Moderate

A weakness that should be addressed but does not invalidate the strategy.

### Minor

A limited improvement or clarification.

For each finding, specify:

- Finding ID.
- Severity.
- Category.
- Description.
- Supporting evidence.
- Strategic implication.
- Confidence.
- Recommended corrective action.
- Whether additional research is required.

## Phase 15. Provide a Verdict

Choose one of the following verdicts:

### Approve

No material weaknesses remain that prevent implementation.

### Approve with Conditions

The strategy is viable, but specific conditions must be satisfied before implementation or scaling.

### Revise

Material weaknesses require changes to the strategy.

### Reconsider

The strategic direction itself may be incorrect, and alternative strategies should be reconsidered.

The verdict must be justified by the findings.

Do not approve a strategy simply because it is well written.

## Output

Produce a structured critic report containing:

1. Strategy summary.
2. Overall verdict.
3. Key strengths.
4. Critical findings.
5. Major findings.
6. Moderate findings.
7. Minor findings.
8. Evidence weaknesses.
9. Critical assumptions.
10. Customer and market challenges.
11. Competitive challenges.
12. Feasibility challenges.
13. Economic challenges.
14. KPI and measurement issues.
15. Failure scenarios.
16. Strongest counterstrategy.
17. Scenario analysis.
18. Prioritized corrective actions.
19. Conditions required for approval.
20. Unresolved questions.

## Handoff

Provide the revision stage with:

- Original strategy.
- Overall verdict.
- Findings ranked by severity.
- Evidence supporting each finding.
- Required corrections.
- Optional improvements.
- Counterstrategy.
- Critical assumptions.
- Additional research requirements.
- Approval conditions.
- Findings that must not be ignored.

The critic must not modify the strategy.

Its responsibility is to independently identify and explain weaknesses that the revision stage must address.
